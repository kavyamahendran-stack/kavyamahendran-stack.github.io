Project 01 · Fraud analytics & SQL

Which card payments are most likely to be fraud?

Data881,441 card transactions
PeriodJan to Dec 2025
Fraud rate1.06% (9,368 cases)
StackSQL (SQLite) · Python · SVG dashboard
View the dashboard Read the SQL

Fraud is rare, which is what makes it hard to catch: almost everything an analyst looks at is genuine. I wanted to find the signals that separate fraud from normal spending, then test whether simple rules built on those signals could catch most of it without flooding a team with false alarms.

Real bank data is confidential, so I used 881,441 simulated transactions from 999 customers, generated with Sparkov, the open-source simulator behind the well-known Kaggle fraud dataset. I loaded them into SQLite and did all the analysis in SQL, using GROUP BY, CASE WHEN, a JOIN to a customers table, CTEs and the LAG window function.

Checking the data first

The first load failed because 117,209 transaction IDs were used more than once. Comparing the repeated rows showed they were different transactions sharing an ID, not double payments, so deleting them would have thrown away 13% of the data. I gave every transaction a new unique ID and re-checked: zero duplicates, and no missing amounts, times, categories or labels. I also dropped names, addresses and card numbers, and gave each customer an anonymous ID.

What separates fraud from genuine spending

  • Time
    Fraud runs at about 0.2% during the day and jumps to 3 to 5% between 22:00 and 03:59.
  • Amount
    Over a third of payments between $500 and $999 were fraud. The average fraud was $533 against $69 for a genuine payment, so fraud was 1.06% of transactions but 7.7% of the money.
  • Velocity
    Payments made within 10 minutes of the customer's previous one were about five times riskier than those 1 to 6 hours apart.
  • Category
    Online shopping, online miscellaneous and in-store grocery had the highest fraud rates.

Testing detection rules

RuleAlerts/dayPrecisionRecallValue caught
Amount ≥ $20014913.2%76.5%98.9%
Night (22:00 to 03:59)5743.8%85.0%84.6%
High-risk category5222.9%59.0%64.4%
Amount ≥ $200 and night4934.0%65.1%83.8%
Amount ≥ $200 and high-risk category4926.4%50.1%64.1%

Precision: share of alerts that were really fraud. Recall: share of all fraud caught. Value caught: share of the money lost to fraud that the rule would have flagged.

Each signal on its own raises too many alerts. Combining amount and time cut them to 49 a day, about 2% of transactions, while still catching 65% of fraud cases and 84% of the money lost, with one alert in three being real fraud.

What I would do differently

  • 01
    Set the rule on earlier months and test it on later ones, so it is not graded on the same data it was built from.
  • 02
    Treat the rates as patterns, not real-world levels. In this simulated data, 975 of 999 customers had a fraud episode, far more than in real life.
  • 03
    Add a velocity rule and compare the rules with a machine learning model trained on the same features.

Project 02 · Machine learning & generative AI

What drives the price of an Airbnb listing in New York?

Data47,660 NYC listings
Features231 after encoding
EnvironmentAWS SageMaker & S3
StackPython · scikit-learn · FAISS

Short-term rental hosts set prices largely by instinct. I wanted to know whether listing attributes alone could explain enough of the variation in price to be useful, and which of those attributes carry the most weight.

I took the public New York City Airbnb dataset into an AWS environment, storing it in S3 and working in SageMaker notebooks. Cleaning meant handling missing values, dropping identifier columns with no predictive value, encoding categoricals and scaling numerics. The modelling set that came out of it held 47,660 listings across 231 features.

Model comparison

ModelMAE ($)RMSE ($)R²
Linear regression42.4262.080.462
Decision tree52.9881.300.077
K-nearest neighbours (k=7)41.6161.970.464

80:20 train/test split. KNN and linear regression are effectively tied. The decision tree was left unconstrained and overfits badly, which the gap between its training and test performance makes plain.

KNN's advantage over linear regression is noise. At 0.464 against 0.462 the two are tied, so linear regression wins on interpretability and speed, and KNN's prediction cost grows with the dataset. The exploratory analysis showed room type and borough as the clearest price drivers: entire homes and Manhattan listings sit well above the rest.

Market segmentation

Alongside the predictive work, I used K-Means to group listings by price, review activity, minimum stay and year-round availability. Four clusters gave the clearest separation:

Cluster 0
Heavily reviewed, consistently active listings. This is the high-demand core of the market.
Cluster 1
Low availability and little review activity, averaging $122.77. Occasional or seasonal hosts.
Cluster 2
Minimum stays averaging over 400 nights, with almost no reviews. These are not genuine short-term rentals. Most are probably dormant listings, or hosts using minimum-stay settings to stay visible without taking bookings.
Cluster 3
The highest average prices combined with high year-round availability. These rooms are expensive and rarely booked, which was the clearest commercial signal in the analysis. They are priced above what the market will pay.

Retrieval-augmented document assistant

The second half of the project built a question-answering system over three publicly available PDF documents. I split the text into 1,000-character chunks with 200-character overlaps, producing 1,154 chunks, converted each to a vector embedding using the text-embedding-3-small model, and stored them in a FAISS vector database for semantic search. Every answer was logged with its source document, chunk IDs and token counts, so it could be checked against the original text.

I then compared one-shot, few-shot and step-by-step prompting on the same question and the same retrieved chunks, judging each on answer quality and token cost. Step-by-step gave the most structured and detailed answer, at 1,129 tokens against 937 for one-shot, so roughly 20% more cost for a clearly better answer. That is a trade-off worth stating openly.

What I would do differently

  • 01
    Constrain the decision tree. Running it unpruned, with no depth or leaf-size limit, guaranteed the overfitting the results show. A bounded tree or a random forest would be the obvious next step.
  • 02
    Treat Cluster 2 as a data-quality finding instead of a market segment. A 400-night minimum stay says more about how the platform is being used than about guest demand.
  • 03
    An R² near 0.46 means more than half the variation in price sits outside the features available. Location at a finer grain than borough, and photograph quality, are the obvious missing variables.

Project 03 · Business intelligence

Why are people leaving? An attrition dashboard for managers

SubjectConnectLab (HR case study)
ToolPower BI
AudienceManagers and HR leads
ModuleMSc Business Intelligence

Every employee who leaves costs a business in recruiting, onboarding and lost knowledge. ConnectLab knew some people were leaving but not where, or why. I built a Power BI dashboard so managers could see where attrition was concentrated and act on it before it turned into a bigger problem.

Preparing the data

The HR data needed work before it could be trusted. I removed duplicate employee records, excluded rows with missing or invalid values in the attrition, department and job role fields, and standardised department names, job titles and Yes/No attrition labels into one consistent format. Without that step, the same department could appear under two names and the rates would be wrong.

What the dashboard shows

The layout moves from the big picture to the detail. It opens with overall attrition, then shows attrition rates by department and by job role, normalised so departments of different sizes can be compared fairly. Below that, it breaks attrition down by salary quartile and overtime, by years since last promotion, and by age and gender.

Findings

  • 01
    Attrition was highest among staff in the lowest salary quartile who also worked overtime. Better-paid staff without overtime were far less likely to leave, so pay and workload need to be looked at together.
  • 02
    The longer someone went without a promotion, the more likely they were to leave. Clearer career paths are a practical lever.
  • 03
    Turnover clustered in particular departments and roles, so targeted retention plans for high-risk roles would do more than one company-wide programme.

Project 04 · Data-driven decision making

Could Hertz's bankruptcy have been seen coming?

SubjectHertz Global Holdings
Period2017 to 2020
DataOrbis · MarketLine · SEC 10-K
StackPython · financial ratios · regression

Hertz filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in May 2020, and the usual story blames COVID. I wanted to test that story against the numbers: was the business healthy until the pandemic hit, or were the warning signs already there?

Data and approach

I combined financial data from the Orbis database, MarketLine industry profiles and Hertz's 2019 and 2020 annual reports and 10-K filing. The preparation followed five steps: collection, quality check, standardisation, grouping ratios into profitability, liquidity, solvency and operations, and trend analysis. I then worked through the analysis in three layers: descriptive (what happened), diagnostic (why) and predictive (what was coming).

What the numbers showed

IndicatorFinding
Revenue, 2019 to 2020$9.78bn to $5.26bn (down 46%)
Net result, 2020$1.7bn loss
Total liabilities, pre-pandemicOver $19bn
Shareholders' equity, 2020Minus $9.2bn
Current ratioBelow 1.0 throughout

Sources: Orbis, Hertz annual reports 2019 and 2020, SEC 10-K.

The pandemic did not create Hertz's problems. It exposed them. Revenue looked stable through 2019, but that hid shrinking margins, heavy debt taken on to fund the fleet, and a current ratio that meant Hertz could not cover its short-term obligations. A cost base built on owned vehicles, airport leases and maintenance could not shrink when demand disappeared.

The forecast

A linear regression on the 2017 to 2020 revenue trend projected continued decline without a change in strategy. The ratios were flashing warnings by 2018, two years before the bankruptcy. With forecasting built into decision-making, there was time to restructure debt and cut costs.

Recommendations

  • 01
    Financial risk management: reduce reliance on debt, build liquidity reserves, and set solvency KPI thresholds that trigger action.
  • 02
    Cost agility: size the fleet dynamically using demand data and cut underused assets in quiet periods.
  • 03
    Predictive analytics: real-time financial dashboards, forecasting built into planning cycles, and scenario planning for shocks.

Project 05 · Strategy & operations

Why a data-sophisticated retailer still failed on food waste

SubjectTesco PLC
FormatMSc business research project
MethodsPESTEL · Five Forces · VRIO
AssessmentWritten report and viva defence

Tesco is among the most data-capable retailers in Britain. It has decades of loyalty-card behaviour, sophisticated demand forecasting and a mature commercial analytics function. It also has a public record of falling short on food waste. My project asked how both of those things can be true at once.

The argument I arrived at: Tesco's commercial data sophistication has not been converted into operational data governance. The capability exists where it drives revenue and is markedly thinner where it would drive waste reduction. That is a structural problem, and it explains why the target slipped while the forecasting stayed where it was.

Approach

I built the competitive picture using PESTEL, Porter's Five Forces, a VRIO analysis of Tesco's resources and a comparison with five UK rivals: Sainsbury's, Aldi, Lidl, Asda and Morrisons. I then tracked Tesco's financial ratios year on year from its annual report. Revenue grew to £73.7bn in FY2025/26, but adjusted operating margin slipped from 4.5% to 4.3% and ROCE from 14.6% to 14.4%, as Tesco invested in price and market share.

The food waste picture centred on the 2024 surplus food scandal, when edible surplus was not properly redistributed and Tesco ended its contract with a waste processor. Tesco has cut operational food waste by 24%, but pushed its 50% reduction target from 2025 to 2030. For the corporate responsibility dimension I worked through Freeman's stakeholder theory, Carroll's CSR pyramid, the Triple Bottom Line and Suchman's legitimacy theory, and mapped six stakeholder groups by their interests, how the issue affected them and Tesco's response.

Recommendations

I closed with four recommendations: AI demand forecasting, digital supply chain traceability, a supplier sustainability framework and an ESG performance dashboard. Each was ranked on business impact against implementation effort and given an owner, a 6 to 18 month timeline, a KPI and a risk assessment. A recommendation nobody can execute is not a recommendation.

The work was defended in a viva, so the argument had to hold up under questioning as well as on the page.

Project 06 · Engineering capstone

Holding a hybrid solar, wind and battery grid steady

ContextBE final-year project, 2025
TeamTwo engineers
Built withPower electronics, Arduino
PublishedTIJER, February 2025

Solar and wind do not produce steady power. A grid needs steady power. My final-year project built a system that takes solar, wind and battery storage, conditions all three into one stable supply, converts it to grid-compatible AC, and monitors the whole thing for faults.

Each source needs different treatment. Solar runs through a SEPIC converter, which can step voltage either up or down depending on what the panels are producing. Ours took 20V in and held 37.8V out. Wind arrives as AC and goes through a dedicated AC to DC converter. The battery sits on a bidirectional converter so it can charge when generation exceeds demand and discharge when it falls short. All three feed a single-phase inverter, and a proportional-integral control algorithm holds the voltage steady across the lot.

The energy management logic decides, moment to moment, whether the solar converter should run at its maximum power point or back off. That depends on three things at once: what the renewables are generating, what the load is asking for, and how much charge the battery is holding.

The monitoring layer

A phasor measurement unit samples the amplitude and phase angle of voltage and current at points across the grid, time-stamped by GPS so readings from different locations can be lined up. The data arrives fast and in volume, which is the case for machine learning. The design applies regression models, decision trees and neural networks to that stream for anomaly detection, fault classification and load forecasting. The accuracy bar for synchrophasor measurement is a total vector error below one percent in steady state.

Worth being clear about the split. The part we built and measured was the power conversion hardware and its control loop. The machine learning monitoring layer was designed and simulated, not deployed against live grid data.

The work was published in TIJER (ISSN 2349-9249), Volume 12 Issue 2, February 2025.

Project 07 · Embedded systems

Two sensor systems that act on what they measure

Both of these are undergraduate Arduino builds. They are small, but they are where I first worked with live sensor data and had to decide what a system should do when a reading crosses a threshold.

Water leak detection and shut-off

A four-person project on preventing water loss in pipework. Ultrasonic and flow sensors watch the line. When the system detects a leak it closes the valve on the affected section and reports the flow rate, so a problem gets caught before it becomes damage. It also reads pH, which turns the same rig into a basic water quality monitor. We scoped a GSM extension to text the location of a leak to whoever maintains the network, though we did not build it.

Soil moisture and automated irrigation

Moisture sensors sit in the soil at several points. An Arduino reads them continuously, and when moisture drops below a set threshold it triggers a relay that opens the pump or valve until the soil is back in range. The point is that irrigation responds to what the ground actually needs instead of running on a timer, which is where most water in conventional irrigation gets wasted.
